The Problem: A 170-Year-Old Device That Medicine Never Replaced

The plaster cast (석고붕대) was introduced in 1851 by Dutch military surgeon Antonius Mathijsen. In the 175 years since, it has become one of the most recognizable medical devices on Earth — and one of the least improved. When a patient fractures a bone today, the clinical procedure is nearly identical to what a field medic would have done in the 19th century: wet strips of plaster-impregnated bandage are wrapped around the limb, left to set for 15–20 minutes, and worn for 6–8 weeks.

The problems with this approach are well-documented and widely tolerated. Traditional plaster is heavy, typically adding 1–3 kg to the affected limb. It is not waterproof: patients cannot shower without elaborate wrapping rituals and risk mold, skin maceration, and cast disintegration if moisture penetrates. It is opaque, meaning clinicians cannot visually inspect the skin beneath without removing the cast — which requires cutting and re-casting, adding clinical cost and patient discomfort. And it is not custom-fit: the wrapping process approximates the limb shape, but gaps between cast and skin create pressure points, friction, and, in severe cases, pressure ulcers.

Fiberglass casts (arrived in the 1970s) addressed the weight issue — they are roughly 40% lighter than plaster — but reproduced the same fundamental constraints: non-waterproof, opaque, not breathable, not custom-fit. After five decades, fiberglass remains the current clinical standard. The orthopedic casting market has not had a meaningful technological inflection since Gerald Scholten and 3M introduced synthetic casting tape in 1975.

This is the market that Park Jong-chil (박종칠) and OpenM (오픈엠) decided to disrupt — with 10 years of R&D, a materials science breakthrough, and a product called OpenCast™.

The Product: OpenCast™ — Open, Lightweight, Waterproof, Custom-Fit

OpenCast™ is an open-lattice thermoplastic orthopedic cast. The "open" in the name is literal: the cast material is perforated — a mesh-like structure with gaps throughout — rather than a solid shell. This single design decision unlocks properties that solid plaster and fiberglass cannot replicate.

The product comes as a 20cm × 40cm sheet of thermoplastic polymer, packaged and stored at room temperature. Application is a straightforward clinical workflow. The sheet is placed in warm water heated to approximately 70°C — the activation temperature — where it becomes soft and malleable within 90 seconds. The clinician removes it from the water, positions it around the patient's limb, and shapes it to the exact contour of that specific anatomy. Within approximately 5 minutes, the material re-hardens at body temperature into a rigid, load-bearing cast that mirrors the patient's limb geometry precisely. Excess material is pinned and trimmed. No additional equipment is needed beyond a warm-water basin.

The clinical properties that result from this process are materially different from anything traditional casting offers. OpenCast™ is waterproof — patients shower, swim, and bathe normally. It is breathable — air circulates through the lattice, eliminating the trapped-moisture environment that causes skin breakdown and odor in traditional casts. It is skin-visible — clinicians can inspect the underlying tissue at any time without cast removal. It is lightweight — the open-lattice structure uses significantly less material than equivalent solid casts. And it is custom-fit — because the material conforms to each patient's specific limb geometry while malleable, the cast follows individual anatomy rather than approximating it.

"We want to make the open cast the new standard." — CEO Park Jong-chil (박종칠) ("우리는 플랫폼이 되어 새로운 시장을 만드는 것이 목표")

The Technology: Thermoplastic Lattice Engineering

The engineering beneath OpenCast™ is more complex than the application workflow suggests. The central challenge is a material science trade-off: a thermoplastic polymer must be rigid enough at body temperature to immobilize a fractured limb, yet soft enough at 70°C to be safely handled and shaped in a clinical setting. It must set within 5 minutes at body temperature, not drift or deform once hardened, and maintain structural integrity when wet. And it must achieve all of this in an open-lattice geometry — which introduces stress concentration points that a solid sheet does not have.

OpenM spent the years from 2007 through 2017 developing and testing the specific polymer compound and lattice geometry that meets all of these requirements simultaneously. The lattice structure itself required iterative optimization — cell size, wall thickness, and hole geometry each affect both mechanical stiffness (too flexible and the cast fails to immobilize; too rigid and it cannot be shaped) and clinical properties (airflow, visibility, weight). The 5,000-patient beta test that preceded commercial launch was not marketing — it was the final phase of clinical validation for a materials engineering problem.

One under-appreciated advantage of the thermoplastic approach is the absence of the exothermic reaction associated with traditional plaster. Plaster of Paris (calcium sulfate hemihydrate) generates heat as it sets — a chemical reaction that can cause thermal burns in thick applications, particularly in pediatric patients. OpenCast™ sets by physical hardening as temperature drops from 70°C to body temperature, with no heat generated. The clinical risk profile is fundamentally different.

The June 2024 launch of "OpenCast Short Leg" — a variant optimized for lower leg and ankle fractures — indicates that OpenM is now extending the product line across specific anatomical indications, a strategy consistent with building a platform product family rather than a single SKU.

The 3D-printed cast comparison is particularly important for the investor narrative. Several well-funded startups (Cortex, Xkelet, and others) have attempted to bring custom orthopedic casts to market via scan-to-print workflows. The approach produces excellent results in the lab — anatomically precise, lightweight, breathable — but has encountered a consistent commercialization barrier: the gap between the point of injury and the point of cast delivery. A patient presenting at an emergency department with a fresh fracture cannot wait 24–48 hours for a printed cast to arrive. OpenCast™ delivers most of the functional advantages of a 3D-printed cast in a workflow that takes 5 minutes and requires no specialized equipment beyond warm water.

Business Model: Platform, Not Single Product

OpenM describes itself as a platform company — a framing that becomes legible once you understand the distribution model. OpenCast™ is not sold directly to patients or even primarily to hospitals as a consumable. Instead, OpenM provides the sheet material and know-how to certified partner clinicians and rehabilitation specialists, who then apply the cast as a service. Partners sell the application service and absorb the material cost; OpenM earns revenue from materials supplied to partners and shares in the value created downstream.

This model has several structural advantages. It aligns OpenM's incentives with clinical adoption: the faster OpenCast™ spreads across the partner network, the more material volume OpenM sells. It creates a recurring revenue stream from each partner relationship — because every fracture patient who walks through a partner clinic's door represents a potential OpenCast™ application. And it builds a defensible distribution moat: as partners train their clinical staff on the application technique and integrate OpenCast™ into their workflow, switching costs increase.

The platform framing also extends to what OpenM calls "application development." The thermoplastic sheet material that forms OpenCast™ can be shaped into forms beyond traditional casts — splints, braces, custom orthotic supports, rehabilitation positioning devices. Partners who develop proprietary applications on top of the base material create new use cases that OpenM captures through material supply. CEO Park Jong-chil's stated ambition is not merely to sell a better cast — it is to become the infrastructure on which Korean rehabilitation medicine's customization layer is built.

Go-to-Market: Hospital-First via 한림MS Distribution

OpenM's commercial launch in June 2022 was structured around a distribution partnership with 한림MS (Hallim MS), a Korean medical supply distributor with existing hospital channel relationships. This decision reflects a classic medtech go-to-market logic: rather than building direct hospital sales force from scratch, leverage a partner with established trust relationships to accelerate clinical adoption during the critical early commercialization phase.

The hospital-first strategy was deliberate. Orthopedic casting decisions in Korea are made by attending physicians and rehabilitation specialists — not purchasing departments. Clinical champion acquisition is the primary sales motion: one orthopedic chief willing to pilot OpenCast™ in their department can generate data, testimonials, and peer referrals that a marketing campaign cannot replicate. OpenM's 70+ hospital deployments as of the research date represent 70 clinical champions who have converted to the product — a foundation for the network effects the platform model requires.

The national health insurance coverage (선별급여) achieved in August 2019 — three years before the commercial launch — was a strategic priority that preceded commercial distribution. Without NHI coverage, patient adoption would require out-of-pocket payment for a premium product in a market where the insured alternative (plaster, fiberglass) is effectively free. With 선별급여, OpenCast™ is reimbursable, removing the primary economic barrier for patient adoption and making hospital adoption a clinical decision rather than a budget one.

Market Opportunity: ₩600B to ₩1T Domestically, $2.5B+ Globally

Orthopedic fracture casting is a high-volume, mature market with a structural technology gap — the kind of setup that typically rewards the first mover who solves the gap with a clinically deployable product rather than a laboratory demonstration. OpenM estimates the Korean domestic addressable market at 1 million fracture patients per year. At 3–5% market penetration, that translates to ₩600B to ₩1T (approximately $450M to $750M) in domestic revenue potential at current pricing and reimbursement levels.

The global addressable market is approximately 100 million fracture patients annually, based on World Health Organization fracture incidence data. The global orthopedic casting market is estimated at $2.5B–$3.5B depending on methodology. Within that total, the thermoplastic segment is the fastest-growing category — driven by the same patient experience demands (waterproof, breathable, lighter) that OpenCast™ directly addresses. OpenM's early export revenue across 9 countries suggests the product translates across clinical contexts with minimal modification.

The pediatric fracture segment deserves specific mention. Children account for a disproportionate share of fracture patients globally (roughly 30% of all fractures occur in patients under 18, with Colles' and greenstick fractures overrepresented). Pediatric patients are also the population where traditional casting causes the most friction: kids swim, play, and sweat; they resist the activity restrictions traditional casts impose; and the appearance and social stigma of a large plaster cast is a documented quality-of-life issue. OpenCast™'s waterproof, breathable, lower-profile design addresses each of these pediatric-specific pain points. A hospital orthopedics department that adopts OpenCast™ for one indication frequently expands to pediatric applications next.

The Thesis: Infrastructure for the Next Generation of Orthopedic Care

OpenM is not primarily a hardware company — it is a materials science company that has solved a clinical problem that the incumbent materials (plaster, fiberglass) cannot solve, and has packaged the solution into a workflow that fits directly into existing clinical practice without requiring new equipment, new training, or new reimbursement pathways.

The 10-year R&D horizon that preceded launch is both a risk and a signal. It is a risk because it represents significant capital consumed before revenue. It is a signal because it reflects the genuine engineering difficulty of the problem: if a waterproof, breathable, custom-fit cast were easy to make, it would have existed before 2022. The difficulty of the problem is the moat. The 11 domestic and 34 international patents reflect legal protection of a specific, hard-won solution to a specific, hard-won engineering challenge.

The platform business model — in which partners apply OpenCast™ rather than patients purchasing a consumer product — mirrors the logic of B2B medical supply infrastructure. It creates predictable, recurring revenue from consumable material sales, limits OpenM's direct-to-patient marketing cost, and aligns the company's incentives with the clinical adoption rate. As the number of certified partner clinicians grows, the material volume grows proportionally. The analogy is not to a device company selling a durable product — it is to a platform company selling the substrate on which clinical services are built.

The near-term watch signal for OpenM is the product line extension trajectory. OpenCast Short Leg (launched June 2024) is the first evidence of SKU proliferation. If the company moves systematically across anatomical indications — wrist (Colles' fracture), pediatric forearm, shoulder immobilizer, ankle — while maintaining the same platform distribution model, the market coverage expands without requiring a fundamental change in clinical relationships or manufacturing infrastructure. Each new variant is a new revenue stream on an existing partner network.

  • NHI 선별급여 coverage transforms OpenCast™ from a premium option to a reimbursed alternative — the single most important commercial de-risking milestone for any Korean medical device.
  • 70+ hospital deployments pre-scale represents a clinical champion network that traditional MedTech distribution takes years to build from scratch.
  • Export revenue of $494,910 before domestic launch completion signals international demand pull — the product travels without significant localization.
  • The platform model (recurring material supply to partner clinicians) creates a revenue profile more similar to SaaS infrastructure than hardware — high gross margin on consumable material, low marginal cost per additional partner.
  • CEO Park Jong-chil's stated ambition to "become the platform" and "create a new market" indicates founder vision calibrated to category creation, not market share capture within an existing segment.

Three Korean MedTech and Deep Tech Startups to Watch This Week

Beyond OpenM, the broader Korean startup ecosystem is producing a cluster of companies in healthcare robotics, wearable diagnostics, and industrial automation that are worth tracking. Here are three that surfaced this week.

LN Robotics (엘앤로보틱스) Medical Robotics
AI-assisted robotic navigation for cardiovascular intervention
LN Robotics is developing an assistant robot system for cardiovascular catheterization procedures. The device assists interventional cardiologists in navigating catheters through coronary and peripheral vasculature — a procedure that currently depends heavily on individual physician skill and exposes operating teams to cumulative radiation from fluoroscopic imaging. The robotic assistance layer reduces operator radiation dose, improves navigation consistency, and opens the possibility of remote or teleoperated cardiac procedures for patients in underserved geographies. Korea's cardiovascular intervention market is substantial: coronary artery disease remains the leading cause of death, and catheterization volume has grown consistently. LN Robotics is positioned at the intersection of robotics, AI-assisted imaging, and structural heart disease — a category where global incumbents (Intuitive, Corindus/Siemens) have focused on the US and European markets, leaving Korean clinical development space open for a local champion.
Watch signal: Cardiovascular intervention robotics is one of the highest-value niches in surgical robotics. Clinical validation partnerships with top-tier Korean cardiology centers (Seoul National University Hospital, Asan Medical Center) would be the key milestone to watch.
Sky Labs (스카이랩스) Wearable Diagnostics · MFDS Approved
Ring-type wearable for continuous blood pressure monitoring — approved 2023
Sky Labs developed CART-I, a ring-form wearable that continuously monitors blood pressure using photoplethysmography (PPG)-based cuffless measurement. The device received MFDS (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety) approval in 2023 — making it one of the first cuffless continuous blood pressure monitors to achieve regulatory clearance anywhere in the world, ahead of more publicized competitors in the US and Europe. Continuous blood pressure monitoring has been a clinical and consumer wearable holy grail for years: hypertension is the leading modifiable cardiovascular risk factor globally, and intermittent cuff measurements catch only a snapshot of a highly variable parameter. A validated, comfortable, ring-form device that passively monitors BP 24/7 changes hypertension management fundamentally — enabling medication titration, white-coat hypertension identification, and nocturnal dipping detection in a way that clinical cuff measurements cannot. Sky Labs' MFDS approval is a genuine regulatory first-mover advantage.
Watch signal: The gap between MFDS approval (Korea) and FDA clearance (US) is the primary commercial scale limiter. An FDA 510(k) or De Novo submission filing would signal readiness for the market where continuous BP monitoring has the largest commercial addressable population.
Novatech (노바테크) Series A · ₩70억
Industrial robot orchestration platform for smart factory environments
Novatech builds software that orchestrates heterogeneous fleets of industrial robots across smart factory environments — a coordination layer that sits above individual robot controllers and optimizes task allocation, path planning, and failure recovery across multiple robot types from multiple vendors. Korea's manufacturing sector is one of the most robotically dense in the world (the country consistently ranks in the top 3 globally for robot density per manufacturing worker), and the transition from fixed-line automation to flexible multi-robot cells is generating significant demand for exactly the kind of orchestration middleware Novatech provides. The company raised ₩70억 (approximately $5M) in a Series A, indicating institutional conviction in the market thesis. The orchestration layer is the software equivalent of the integration problem in cloud infrastructure: as factory automation becomes heterogeneous and dynamic, the value migrates from individual device capability to the intelligence that coordinates the fleet.
Watch signal: Enterprise software in Korean manufacturing scales through large integrator partnerships (Hyundai, Samsung, LG facilities integrators). A named anchor customer in a tier-1 Korean conglomerate's manufacturing network would validate the enterprise go-to-market motion.
