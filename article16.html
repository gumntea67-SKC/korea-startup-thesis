The Problem: Why Factory Inspection Is Still Mostly Human

Modern manufacturing lines are a paradox. They use extraordinarily precise machines to build extraordinarily precise products — semiconductor chips, consumer electronics, electric vehicle components — and then rely on extraordinarily imprecise methods to verify that those products meet quality standards. The final inspection stage at most production facilities still depends heavily on human judgment: trained workers checking products by eye, by hand, or with simple camera systems that can only detect surface defects under controlled lighting conditions.

The problem isn't that robots can't inspect. It's that robots break down when production environments change. Automated inspection systems typically rely on fixed-position cameras and pre-programmed motion sequences. If a component shifts slightly on the conveyor belt, if a product variant is slightly larger or smaller than the calibrated model, if the ambient lighting changes — the system fails. The robot either misses defects or flags correct parts as defective. Manufacturers then have to halt the line, recalibrate, or revert to human inspection. In high-volume electronics manufacturing — where LG Electronics alone produces tens of millions of devices per year — even a 1% inspection failure rate has enormous consequences.

The deeper problem is software, not hardware. Modern robotic arms are mechanically capable of extraordinary precision. The missing link is intelligence: a system that can look at a product that's slightly out of position, understand its geometry in three dimensions, recalculate the robot's movement path in real time, and execute a precise inspection sequence — all without human intervention. This is the gap IRVIS (아일비스) was built to close.

The Founding Team: A Rare Combination of Engineering, AI, and Industrial Sales

IRVIS was founded in November 2024 and is headquartered in Gwanak-gu, Seoul — specifically at Sillim-ro 117, a building within Seoul National University's Campus Town innovation ecosystem. The company's location is no coincidence: its Technical Advisor, Taewan Kim (김태완), is a professor in Seoul National University's Department of Naval Architecture and Ocean Engineering, and IRVIS's foundational relationship with SNU underpins both its R&D pipeline and its talent network.

Gukwon Chung (정국원), CEO, holds an MSc from the University of Birmingham and brings hands-on experience managing robotic automation projects in industrial settings. His background as a project manager — rather than a pure researcher — shapes how IRVIS operates: focused on field-ready solutions, not laboratory prototypes. The company's entire go-to-market approach reflects his orientation: don't pitch a concept, build a system that works on the factory floor and let the results speak.

Camille Kesseler, CTO, completed a Master's degree in AI Vision at Seoul National University — an unusual profile, combining European technical education with SNU's AI research environment. Her expertise in bridging AI perception and robotic control is the technical core of IRVIS's differentiation: the ability to make robots "see" production environments intelligently rather than mechanically.

The team's international dimension extends beyond the founding founders. The engineering team includes Antoine Pateyron (precision mechanism design, ECAM Lyon), Ilias El Abbassi (robotic control and embedded systems, Lille University), and Mathys Vinatier (AI and robotic control, IPSA). This level of international technical talent composition at a seed-stage Korean company is unusual — and deliberately so. IRVIS's Paris office and HEC Incubation Program selection signal that global expansion is a design principle, not an afterthought.

Rounding out the leadership: Seungwoo Han, Managing Director, brings 20+ years of global sales and marketing experience in materials science and engineering; and Hanjung Kwon, General Manager, holds an MBA from the University of South Dakota and manages the financial foundation for stable growth.

The Technology: Vision-Guided Robot Control That Adapts to Change

IRVIS's core technology stack has two interdependent components: Vision-Guided Robot Control and QC Automation Software. Together, they solve the recalibration problem that makes conventional industrial inspection automation fragile.

Vision-Guided Robot Control is built on coordinate transformation technology. IRVIS's system uses a camera to capture the position, orientation, and geometry of a target object — and then converts that visual information into the robot's control coordinate system in real time. This means the robot can precisely position its end-effector to inspect or test a part even when that part's position has shifted, its size varies within tolerance, or it belongs to a different product variant than the last run. The three technical pillars are: Coordinate Transformation (precise 3D scene-to-robot-space conversion), Adaptive Positioning (real-time adjustment to object variation), and Precision Calibration (sub-millimeter accuracy maintenance over time).

QC Automation Software provides the inspection workflow layer: standardizing complex quality control sequences in software so they can be deployed consistently across multiple production lines and sites, logging and integrating with existing manufacturing execution systems (MES), and enabling quality standardization across shift changes, line configurations, and product variants. The practical result for a customer like LG Electronics is that the same inspection logic runs consistently at 2am on a Monday as it does at 2pm on a Friday — without human variance.

The underlying architecture is building toward a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model — a next-generation approach to robot control that enables robots to understand inspection instructions in natural language, map them to visual scene understanding, and generate precise action sequences. This positions IRVIS on the leading edge of what the robotics field calls "foundation model robotics": robots that can be retrained for new inspection tasks through instruction rather than manual reprogramming.

The LG Electronics Partnership: The Best Early Customer a Robotics Startup Can Have

For a company founded in November 2024, IRVIS's commercial traction is exceptional. By February 2025 — three months after founding — the company had signed its first contract with LG Electronics for ESD (Electrostatic Discharge) test automation equipment, and had filed a patent for its core Test Robot System for Device Under Test. The speed from founding to signed contract with a global electronics manufacturer is almost without precedent in Korean deep-tech.

The LG relationship did not stop at one contract. A second agreement followed in June 2025 for an automated audio measurement system. A third in October 2025 for video measurement software automation. And a fourth in April 2026 for integrated management software covering various LG Display QC equipment — extending the relationship from LG Electronics into the LG Display subsidiary and from single-use automation into multi-system management software.

The performance data from the initial LG deployment is what makes this relationship particularly significant. IRVIS's ESD inspection system increased LG's automated measurement success rate from 85% to 98% — a 14-percentage-point improvement. It achieved a test precision error rate within ±1mm. It reduced testing time by 33%. These are not marginal improvements. They represent the kind of performance delta that justifies replacing existing systems and establishing a strategic supplier relationship.

For investors evaluating IRVIS, the LG relationship functions as a proof point that addresses the central risk in early-stage industrial automation startups: the question of whether laboratory performance translates to factory-floor performance under production conditions. IRVIS has answered that question affirmatively, at scale, with one of the world's most demanding electronics manufacturers as its reference customer.

The Dual-Hub Strategy: Seoul and Paris

In April 2026, IRVIS confirmed tenancy at Station F in Paris — the world's largest startup campus, housed in a converted 19th-century railway station in the 13th arrondissement — and was selected for the HEC Incubation Program, run by one of Europe's leading business schools. This development, combined with a research internship agreement with ENS Paris Saclay (signed December 2025), establishes IRVIS as a genuinely dual-market company at the seed stage.

The strategic logic for Europe is straightforward. European manufacturing — particularly in Germany, France, and the Benelux countries — faces a structural inspection automation gap similar to Korea's. Europe's automotive, aerospace, and industrial equipment sectors operate complex quality control requirements with labor-intensive inspection processes. At the same time, European policymakers and manufacturers are actively investing in "Industry 4.0" digital manufacturing — creating both demand and funding programs for exactly the kind of AI-driven inspection automation IRVIS provides.

The HEC and ENS Paris Saclay connections give IRVIS something that most Korean deep-tech startups lack when attempting European expansion: credentialed academic and institutional relationships that signal legitimacy to European enterprise procurement teams. And Station F's network — which includes Amazon, BNP Paribas, LVMH, Microsoft, and dozens of European corporate partners operating accelerator programs on-site — provides direct access to European enterprise decision-makers who could become IRVIS's next reference customers.

From the Seoul HQ, the global expansion is further supported by 2080 Ventures — IRVIS's lead Seed investor, a US-headquartered accelerator and VC operating in 10+ countries including Korea — which provides access to East Asian, ASEAN, and Middle Eastern market networks alongside its capital.

Market: Industrial Inspection Automation Is a Decade-Long Opportunity

The market IRVIS is addressing is large, structurally underserved, and growing faster than most investors appreciate. The global machine vision market — cameras, lighting, and processing systems used for automated inspection — was valued at approximately $9.7 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow to $15+ billion by 2030. But machine vision, on its own, is the passive sensing layer. The larger opportunity lies in intelligent robotics: systems that can act on visual data to perform complex inspection tasks, not just detect surface anomalies.

The AI-driven industrial robotics market — which is what IRVIS participates in — is a rapidly expanding segment within the broader $240+ billion global industrial automation market. Korea's manufacturing sector, which includes some of the world's largest electronics producers (LG Electronics, Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix) and automotive manufacturers (Hyundai, Kia), represents one of the highest-density addressable markets for AI robotic inspection globally: high product volumes, demanding quality requirements, and constant pressure to reduce human labor in quality verification.

Europe represents a second large TAM. Germany's automotive and industrial equipment sectors alone spend billions annually on quality inspection infrastructure. France's aerospace and defense manufacturing (Airbus, Safran, Thales) has similarly demanding QC requirements. IRVIS's Station F presence and HEC connections are not early positioning — they are active market development.

The competitive moat in this market builds quickly. Once a factory's inspection robots are calibrated to IRVIS's vision-guided control system, and once the QC data from those robots is integrated into the factory's MES, switching costs become very high. This is a software-defined hardware business where initial deployment creates durable customer lock-in — the same dynamic that makes industrial software companies like PTC, Siemens PLM, and Rockwell Automation structurally valuable.

Why IRVIS Matters: The Signal Box

Signal Box — Why IRVIS Is Worth Watching
  • LG Electronics is the best possible reference customer for this market: LG produces tens of millions of consumer electronics devices annually across TV, appliances, and mobile products. Getting three contracts with LG Electronics in a company's first year isn't just impressive — it means IRVIS's technology works on production lines that operate 24/7 at scale. Every subsequent enterprise customer pitch starts with "we already work with LG Electronics."
  • The 85%→98% performance improvement is a decision-maker number: Enterprise procurement is not won with demos. It's won with measurable outcomes. A 14-percentage-point improvement in robot success rate, precision within ±1mm, and 33% faster testing are numbers that finance departments can model and manufacturing VPs can approve capital budgets around.
  • Dual-city seed-stage is rare and strategic: Most Korean deep-tech startups wait until Series A or B to attempt European expansion. IRVIS opened a Paris office at Station F as a seed-stage company — because its CTO and several engineers are European, its academic partnerships include ENS Paris Saclay, and European manufacturing is a genuine TAM, not a brand exercise. This is a company that understood its geographic strategy early.
  • The VLA model direction aligns with where industrial robotics is heading: Foundation model robotics — where robots learn from natural language instructions and visual demonstration rather than manual reprogramming — is the most significant long-term shift in industrial automation. IRVIS building toward VLA architecture positions it at the frontier of this transition, not as a lagging adopter.
  • SNU + 2080 Ventures + HEC creates a defensible support network: Korea's government startup support infrastructure (Campus Town, Early-Stage Package), SNU's R&D relationships, 2080 Ventures' global network, and HEC's European enterprise connections together give IRVIS a support ecosystem that is unusually hard to replicate for a company of its stage.
  • ISO 9001:2015 at 13 months signals operational discipline: Most hardware startups don't pursue quality management certification until they're preparing for enterprise RFPs. IRVIS achieving ISO 9001:2015 in December 2025 — just over a year after founding — suggests a management team that understands what enterprise customers require and builds toward those requirements early.

The investment thesis for IRVIS is straightforward: a seed-stage company has achieved extraordinary early commercial traction (three LG Electronics contracts in Year 1), built a technology that demonstrably outperforms incumbents on real production lines (85%→98%), opened a second market (Station F, Paris) before most startups have found their first, and assembled a team with the right combination of engineering depth and commercial credibility to execute on the opportunity.

The primary risk is the one that applies to all hardware startups: scaling from three customer deployments to thirty requires operational infrastructure, supply chain, and service capacity that costs money and takes time to build. The milestones to watch: Series A timing and structure, expansion of the LG relationship into additional LG Group entities (beyond LG Electronics and LG Display), first European customer announcement, and the eventual VLA model deployment on a production line.

At the seed stage, IRVIS is one of the most interesting deep-tech companies we've tracked in Korea — precisely because it moved so fast from founding to meaningful commercial revenue with a global manufacturer as its anchor customer.

Dnotitia designs VDPU (Vector Data Processing Unit) chips optimized for generative AI inference and training workloads — a homegrown Korean alternative to Nvidia's GPU architecture for specific AI compute use cases. The company closed a $61.2M Series A in July 2026, one of the largest single AI semiconductor funding rounds in Korean history. This positions the company in the global AI chip race alongside Groq, Tenstorrent, and Graphcore — but with Korean manufacturing relationships and a domestic customer base that includes Korean AI cloud providers and hyperscalers building AI infrastructure in Asia.
Watch for: First production tape-out milestones, Korean AI cloud customer announcements, and strategic investment from Samsung or SK Hynix as validation of the silicon architecture.
프레이저테라퓨틱스 (Fraizer Therapeutics) Pre-IPO · ₩500억
Targeted protein degradation drugs — a new category of oncology treatment entering Korea's clinical pipeline
Fraizer Therapeutics is developing targeted protein degradation (TPD) therapies — a drug modality that marks disease-causing proteins for destruction by the cell's own proteasome system, rather than blocking them the way conventional drugs do. The approach, using degrader molecules including PROTACs and molecular glues, allows Fraizer to target proteins that have historically been considered "undruggable" — a class that includes many of the most important oncology targets. The company closed a ₩500억 Pre-IPO round in July 2026, signaling preparation for a KOSDAQ listing that would provide liquidity for early investors and additional development capital. Korea's biopharmaceutical sector has produced several successful TPD programs since the field was pioneered by researchers at Yale and the Ciulli lab in Dundee, and Fraizer's large Pre-IPO round reflects both scientific progress and market readiness.
Watch for: KOSDAQ IPO filing, Phase 1 clinical trial results for the lead PROTAC program, and potential licensing discussions with global pharma partners in oncology.
노바테크 (Novatech) Series A · ₩70억
Industrial robot orchestration platform — the software layer that makes multi-robot factories manageable
While IRVIS solves the individual robot inspection problem, Novatech addresses the coordination layer above it: how do factories manage fleets of robots from different manufacturers, running different tasks, on the same production floor? The industrial robot orchestration platform market has emerged as a critical infrastructure need as factories move from single-robot deployments to multi-robot, multi-vendor environments. Novatech's platform allows production managers to schedule, monitor, and optimize robot workflows across heterogeneous fleets — analogous to what Kubernetes does for containerized software, but for factory robots. The ₩70억 Series A in July 2026 reflects growing enterprise demand from Korean automotive and electronics manufacturers who are rapidly expanding robot density but struggling with the software integration complexity this creates. IRVIS and Novatech are complementary: one handles the robot's vision intelligence, the other manages the fleet.
Watch for: Hyundai Motor or Samsung Electronics pilot announcements, integration partnerships with ABB or Fanuc robot hardware vendors, and Series B timing as the fleet management market consolidates.
