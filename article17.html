The Problem: The Hidden Health Crisis in Every Commercial Kitchen

There's a number that Korea's foodservice industry doesn't talk about openly: the rate at which kitchen workers develop lung cancer. The culprit isn't the food. It's the air. Every time a piece of chicken goes into a commercial fryer, the oil doesn't just cook the food — it also burns, oxidizes, and creates a microplume of acrolein, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and other class-1 carcinogens. These compounds rise with the steam, and the people standing over the fryers breathe them continuously for eight to ten hours a day.

Conventional kitchen hood systems can capture some of what rises. They can't stop the fumes at the source. The vapor forms at the oil surface — not three feet above it. By the time a hood system pulls air out of the kitchen, the cook has already inhaled the worst of it. This is a structural problem that the existing ventilation industry has known about for decades and done relatively little to solve, because solving it properly would require changing the physics of frying itself.

The second problem is less visible but equally costly: meat aging. Korean restaurant operators — particularly those serving pork or beef — rely on a process that takes between 5 and 14 days. During that period, enzymes slowly break down muscle fibers, improving tenderness, moisture retention, and flavor depth. The problem is that this process requires refrigerated storage space, ties up working capital for weeks, and is economically viable only for premium cuts. Lower-value cuts (목살, 앞다리, and similar) rarely receive proper aging because the economics don't work — the inventory cost exceeds the margin improvement. So they get sold cheap, under-utilized, with menus built around higher-margin cuts that the market can only absorb in limited quantities.

Both problems — carcinogenic frying and inefficient aging — are structural. They exist not because kitchen operators haven't tried to solve them, but because the tools available have been fundamentally limited. (주)제로원 (ZeroOne) built a different kind of tool.

The SonicWand: One Device, One Physics Principle, Two Extreme Environments

SonicWand™ is the world's only broadband multi-purpose ultrasonic cooking device. That description requires unpacking, because "ultrasonic cooking device" has been a category with exactly one meaningful player — industrial lab equipment that costs more than a commercial kitchen and can't survive a working fry station — until ZeroOne changed the definition.

What makes SonicWand distinctive is the operating range. The device works from −5°C to 220°C. At the cold end, it performs sonic aging in sous-vide-temperature water tanks. At the hot end, it operates fully submerged in frying oil at temperatures that would destroy conventional ultrasonic transducers within seconds. Every competitor in the ultrasonic cooking space operates at a fraction of this range. Most work only in water. None work in high-temperature oil at commercial kitchen durability standards.

The breakthrough that makes this possible is ZeroOne's solution to what engineers call the Curie Temperature problem. Conventional ultrasonic transducers use piezoelectric ceramics that lose their piezoelectric properties when heated above a threshold temperature — the Curie point. At frying temperatures, they simply stop vibrating. ZeroOne's engineering team redesigned the transducer stack, titanium probe geometry, and heat-dissipation system to maintain stable ultrasonic output across a temperature span that no previous commercial product covered.

The hardware itself is designed for the reality of commercial kitchens: waterproof, heat-resistant, low-noise, with a 115mm probe that handles up to 50 liters of oil or water. A separate controller unit provides a 1–9 hour timer and precision intensity adjustment. Installation requires no equipment replacement — the device clips onto existing fryers and water tanks via a jig in under a minute.

"Install the future of the kitchen, with your existing equipment." — ZeroOne product tagline (기존 장비 그대로, 주방의 미래를 설치하십시오)

Core Technology: Cavitation — 35,000 Micro-Explosions Per Second

The mechanism behind SonicWand is cavitation — a physical phenomenon where rapid pressure oscillations cause microscopic bubbles to form and collapse in a liquid. At 35,000Hz, SonicWand generates 35,000 of these bubbles per second. When each bubble collapses, it releases a localized burst of approximately 6,000°C microenergy and a pressure spike roughly 30,000 times atmospheric pressure. These are not figurative numbers. They reflect the known physics of acoustic cavitation, a phenomenon studied extensively in industrial cleaning, pharmaceuticals, and food science for decades.

What ZeroOne did was engineer a device that applies this physics consistently and safely inside commercial cooking environments — which is a fundamentally different challenge from applying it in a laboratory.

In oil, cavitation does two things simultaneously: it rapidly separates water from oil molecules (suppressing the lipid oxidation chain reaction that causes rancidity) and physically disrupts the surface tension that causes oil to splatter and smoke at high temperatures. The result is oil that burns less, spatters less, and lasts longer — while the food that cooks in it absorbs less fat and retains more internal moisture.

In cold water (or vacuum packaging immersed in water), cavitation breaks down muscle fiber connective tissue — specifically the collagen cross-links that make meat tough — without the enzyme degradation that traditional aging relies on. Enzyme-based aging is slow because enzymes work at a fixed biochemical rate. Cavitation is a mechanical process. It can do in 60 minutes what enzymes need 7 days to accomplish, because it's operating at a different physical scale entirely.

Application 1 — Frying (160°C to 220°C): Safer, Crispier, Longer-Lasting

Commercial fryers create a specific set of physics problems that ZeroOne addresses directly.

The first is oil rancidity. At frying temperatures, oil undergoes rapid oxidation — triglycerides break down into free fatty acids, peroxides, and aldehydes. The aldehyde acrolein is particularly dangerous at inhaled concentrations and is the compound primarily responsible for the carcinogenic profile of fry smoke. Cavitation interrupts this chain reaction: the acoustic waves disperse the micro-water droplets in oil quickly, preventing the localized hot spots where oxidation accelerates fastest. KTR (한국건설생활환경시험연구원) testing certified a 75% reduction in cooking vapor. A separate test by the Korea Chemical Fusion Test Institute (한국화학융합시험연구원) confirmed THC (total hydrocarbon) reduction of 80% and TVOC reduction of 47%.

The second is oil viscosity and burn risk. SonicWand's vibration interference lowers oil viscosity, which reduces the likelihood of superheated oil droplets adhering to food surfaces — and to the skin of the cook reaching into the fryer. KHFF (Korea Health Functional Foods) testing confirmed a 28% reduction in oil peroxide value (산패, oxidation index) versus conventional fryers in standard conditions.

The third is food texture. Cavitation creates the crispy-outside, moist-inside effect — known in Korean culinary culture as "겉바속촉" — by suppressing excessive fat absorption while maintaining surface dehydration. Multiple ingredients can be fried simultaneously without flavor transfer because the ultrasonic waves prevent aromatic compounds from migrating between oil zones.

Application 2 — Sonic Aging (−5°C to Low Temp): 7 Days in 60 Minutes

The economics of traditional meat aging are broken for the majority of Korean restaurant operators. Premium aging requires 7–14 days of refrigerated inventory, during which capital is frozen and space is consumed. Only the highest-value cuts can justify this cost. The result: most of a slaughtered pig or cow ends up in less profitable configurations, because the non-premium cuts simply aren't worth aging.

SonicWand's Sonic Aging process uses a four-step protocol:

The economic impact is significant. A restaurant that currently ages 30% of its pork inventory (premium cuts only) can now age 100% of it in the same space, with zero additional time delay. Non-premium cuts become premium-comparable products. Meal kit and HMR (home meal replacement) operators can manufacture aged meat products without maintaining 7–14 days of frozen inventory. ZeroOne estimates investor payback within 6 months for most institutional operators.

The sonic aging capability also extends to sauces and liquors. Three minutes of ultrasonic treatment of soju or wine submerged in water can achieve flavor depth equivalent to extended bottle aging — creating differentiation opportunities for franchise beverage programs.

Market Traction: Schools, Welfare Centers, and National Television

ZeroOne has taken a deliberate institutional-first go-to-market strategy. Rather than pursuing restaurant chains (which are large accounts but slow procurement cycles with complex B2B sales), ZeroOne targeted government-operated kitchens first — nursing homes, elderly welfare centers, and public schools. This was strategic: institutional kitchens have formal procurement infrastructure, risk-averse operators who care deeply about worker health and safety compliance, and budget cycles that ZeroOne's S2B (학교장터) marketplace registration unlocks directly.

Market Opportunity: $253M and a Government Mandate for 30 Unicorns

ZeroOne operates at the intersection of three converging market forces in Korea.

The first is foodtech investment acceleration. Korean agri-food tech startup funding reached $253M in 2025 — a 171% increase from $97M the prior year, per AgFunder's 2026 Global AgriFoodTech Investment Report. The Korean government has formally targeted 30 food tech unicorns by 2027, backed by a ₩100B ($72M) dedicated fund. Agriculture Minister Song Mi-ryung stated in early 2026: "AI is no longer a choice but a core foundation that will determine the survival and future competitiveness of agriculture and rural communities." Policy-driven capital is accelerating into the sector precisely when private capital is also concentrating.

The second is commercial kitchen health and safety regulation. Korea has seen a surge in industrial injury classifications among school cafeteria workers, nursing home kitchen staff, and food processing workers — driven primarily by respiratory conditions attributed to long-term cooking fume exposure. As worker safety litigation increases and regulatory attention intensifies, institutional buyers face growing pressure to demonstrate they are reducing fume exposure proactively. SonicWand's KTR and chemical fusion certifications are exactly the documentation institutional procurement requires.

The third is K-food's global expansion opportunity. Korea's food exports reached $13.62B in 2025 and are targeting $16B by end of 2026. As Korean food concepts scale globally — particularly franchise chicken and processed meat products — the operators building those supply chains need consistent quality standards at scale. SonicWand's sonic aging capability is a direct enabler of that consistency: standardized aging in 60 minutes allows franchise operations to control product quality in ways that 7-14 day natural aging simply cannot replicate across multiple geographies.

The addressable market for commercial kitchen ultrasonic technology is harder to size precisely than consumer electronics — but the institutional kitchen market in Korea alone (schools, hospitals, welfare centers, military, franchises) runs into the hundreds of thousands of kitchen installations. At ₩3–5M per SonicWand unit, and with S2B marketplace access to over 11,000 public schools, the near-term institutional TAM is meaningful even before the restaurant and franchise channels open.

Investment Signals

Why ZeroOne Matters for Global Investors
  • The technology gap is real and documented. No competing product operates across -5°C to 220°C in a commercial kitchen environment. ZeroOne's Curie Temperature engineering is not marketing language — it's a solved physics problem that no other company has solved commercially. The KTR, KAFRI, KHFF, and CE certifications prove performance in the real world, not a lab.
  • The institutional-first GTM de-risks the hardware startup curse. Most hardware startups fail because they can't convert R&D excellence into reliable revenue. ZeroOne built its commercial traction through government procurement channels — S2B registration, public school installations, welfare center deployments — before pursuing the higher-volume but harder restaurant franchise market. Institutional buyers are slow but sticky.
  • Worker health regulation is a structural tailwind. The industrial injury rate in Korean institutional kitchens is rising. Litigation and compliance pressure on school and hospital kitchens will accelerate adoption of documented fume-reduction technology — exactly what SonicWand provides with its certified evidence stack.
  • The Sonic Aging use case opens a second B2B market independently. Meat aging is a genuine operational problem for Korean restaurant operators and HMR manufacturers. The S2B school market and the restaurant/HMR market are parallel acquisition channels that reinforce each other without competing for the same budget cycle.
  • National TV validation created consumer-pull that flows back into B2B. SBS and tvN features are not just brand awareness — they created documented demand spikes at mart-level product installations, which in turn generate inbound inquiries from franchise operators and institutional buyers who saw consumer response before committing procurement budget. This is an unusual demand signal for a B2B hardware company.
  • CE certification signals genuine export readiness. Most Korean food hardware startups are domestically focused. ZeroOne's European EMC certification is not standard practice for a company at this stage — it suggests the team understands that the same kitchen health and safety pressures driving institutional adoption in Korea exist across European institutional foodservice markets.

The investment thesis for ZeroOne is simple: a hardware deeptech company with documented institutional traction, a patented technology breakthrough with no equivalent competitor, certified third-party performance data, government procurement channel access, and a product that addresses regulatory tailwinds in both Korea and export markets. The question is not whether the technology works — the certifications and field deployments answer that. The question is whether the company can scale its institutional sales motion fast enough to build the revenue base required for its next phase. That is a commercial execution question, not a technology question. And commercial execution questions have clearer answers than technology ones.

CES 2026 Innovation Award winner building last-mile delivery for Korea's vertical city reality
GOLE Robotics won a CES 2026 Innovation Award for its AA-2 last-mile delivery robot — a machine that reflects Korea's specific urban environment in ways that competing global delivery robots do not. The company's positioning as a food delivery logistics layer is directly tied to the growth of dark kitchens, HMR subscription services, and franchise delivery expansion across Korean cities.
Why it matters: The CES recognition is early-stage signal, but the elevator integration is a genuine technical moat in the Korean urban context — it's an infrastructure problem that competitors who designed for suburban US environments will have to rebuild from scratch.
